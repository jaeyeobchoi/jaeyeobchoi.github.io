Jaeyeob Choi

I am an undergraduate in the Department of Psychology at Chung-Ang University in Seoul, majoring in psychology with a minor in economics (B.A. expected Summer 2027). I started from the idea that to understand people, you have to understand the brain. The question I keep returning to is how the brain turns experience into emotion, memory, and choice, and where that process goes wrong, as in depression, anxiety, and dementia.

So far I have worked at the level of behavior. In 2023 I led an undergraduate poster team in Sasaek, our department's student academic club: I designed and ran a divided-visual-field reaction-time experiment with eye-tracking, and we prepared for the conference with direct weekly guidance from Prof. Soohyun Cho. Our poster, on which I was first author, received the Undergraduate Poster Excellence Award at the 77th Annual Conference of the Korean Psychological Association. Next I want to look at the neural basis of behavior with neuroimaging, and then explain it with mathematics and computational models. My interests are still broad; I plan to narrow them through graduate training.

Research

The Effect of Stimulus Type on Visual Field Advantages

Undergraduate Poster Excellence Award · Korean Psychological Association, 2023

2023.02 – 2023.08 · Undergraduate Poster Team, academic club Sasaek · Team lead, first author · Faculty advisor: Prof. Soohyun Cho

Does the visual-field (hemispheric) advantage depend on what is being perceived? We predicted that right-handers would respond faster to faces in the left visual field (right hemisphere) and to words in the right visual field (left hemisphere), with both kinds of stimuli rotated 180°.

Method
A divided-visual-field reaction-time task. Fifty-one right-handed college students, screened with the Van Strien (2016) handedness inventory, categorized a stimulus shown for 200 ms to the left or right of fixation: happy vs. afraid faces (272 trials) and Korean words, animal vs. fruit/vegetable (84 trials). A trial counted only when the answer was correct and gaze had stayed on the fixation point, checked with a Gazepoint GP3 eye-tracker.
Result
Responses were faster in the left visual field for rotated faces (paired t = −2.145, p = .039) and in the right visual field for rotated words (t = 2.065, p = .044), as predicted. In a two-way ANOVA the stimulus type × visual field interaction was not significant (F = 0.51, p = .476); only the main effect of stimulus type was (F = 16.96, p < .001). Limitations: only right-handers were tested, and there was no upright (non-rotated) control.
How it was run
A student-club project. The team met twice a week; I designed the eye-tracker-controlled experiment and ran data collection for about two months. We prepared for the conference with direct weekly guidance from Prof. Soohyun Cho.

Materials

Word stimuli. Korean nouns from two categories (animal, fruit/vegetable), shown rotated 180°:

Face stimuli. Happy and afraid faces from the Karolinska Directed Emotional Faces (Lundqvist, Flykt, & Öhman, 1998), rotated 180°. They are not reproduced here, in line with the KDEF terms of use.

Example data. Raw data from one participant (CSV): anonymized trial-level data, 272 trials, before filtering. Columns: visual field, stimulus category, response, response time, and Focus (whether gaze stayed on fixation). Only trials with maintained fixation entered the analysis.
